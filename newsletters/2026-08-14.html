Public Markets 101
Six concepts your portfolio manager and analyst users reference constantly

These terms surface daily in conversations with portfolio managers and analysts. Knowing them precisely will sharpen your product questions and make you credible in the room when it counts.

Short Selling
Borrowing and selling a security you don't own, betting it will fall in price. If it does, you buy it back cheaper and pocket the difference. AI tools that scan for short candidates (weak fundamentals, negative sentiment signals) are a growing edge for hedge funds.
Volatility / VIX
Volatility measures how much a security's price swings. The VIX — the "fear index" — tracks expected 30-day market volatility. High VIX = uncertainty. Portfolio managers monitor it as a risk signal; AI systems now model VIX regime changes in real time.
Yield Curve
A chart plotting interest rates on government bonds from short-term (3 months) to long-term (30 years). An inverted yield curve (short rates higher than long) is historically a recession signal — and a critical input to any fixed-income or macro-oriented AI tool.
Market Capitalisation
Share price × total shares outstanding. The primary measure of company size. Large-cap, mid-cap, and small-cap each behave differently in a portfolio — and AI is increasingly used to screen, cluster, and rank companies within each band at speed.
EPS (Earnings Per Share)
Net profit divided by shares outstanding. The single most-tracked quarterly number for equity analysts. AI tools that ingest earnings call transcripts and model EPS surprises ahead of announcements are among the highest-value use cases in your space.
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortisation. A standardised measure of operating profitability, widely used in valuation and credit analysis. When Gartner projects agentic AI will improve firm EBITDA by 5.4%, this is the number they mean.

EU AI Act · Europe

EU AI Act High-Risk Deadline Has Passed — Banks and Insurers Now Face Binding Legal Consequences

August 2, 2026 was the EU AI Act's enforcement date for high-risk AI systems, covering Articles 9–17 (provider) and Article 26 (deployer). Credit scoring, fraud detection, and insurance underwriting AI are fully in scope. Penalties reach €15M or 3% of global annual turnover. Note: the European Union subsequently delayed the broader compliance deadline to December 2027 — but August 2 obligations remain live for systems already deployed.

Why It Matters

If your firm operates in the EU or serves EU-regulated entities, the AI systems your product touches — credit signals, portfolio recommendations, risk flags — may already fall under binding obligations for bias monitoring, audit trails, and documented risk management. This is not a future concern.

Research & Think About
  • Read: Finextra — "The EU AI Act's August 2026 Deadline: What Financial Services Firms Must Do Now" — the most practical, finance-specific breakdown of what Articles 9–17 require in operational terms
  • Does your product's AI output fall under the "high-risk" definition? The test is whether it materially affects financial decisions about natural persons — run that check against your current use cases
  • Understand Article 13 specifically: it requires automated logging and traceability — ask your engineers whether your system can produce an audit trail that satisfies this requirement today

SEC · US Enforcement

SEC Makes "AI Washing" Its Top 2026 Examination Priority — Six Enforcement Actions Already Filed

The SEC has brought six AI washing enforcement actions to date (totalling $44M+ in alleged fraud), and has declared AI scrutiny a standing examination priority for 2026 through its new Cyber and Emerging Technologies Unit (CETU). Examiners will assess written AI policies under Rule 206(4)-7, substantiation for AI capability claims in marketing, and adequacy of AI recordkeeping under Rules 17a-3 and 17a-4.

Why It Matters

Any AI capability you describe in a product brief, pitch deck, or client-facing material is now a potential enforcement surface if you cannot substantiate it during an exam. The standard your firm's compliance team will apply to your product is: can we prove this claim held up, in writing, under audit?

Research & Think About
  • Read: Reg Intel — "SEC and AI: What Financial Firms Need to Know in 2026" — clear, practical, and current on the six enforcement cases and what they signal about examiner intent
  • Review your product's external-facing materials now: does every AI capability claim have an internal document that substantiates it with evidence, not just assertions?
  • Understand what "AI recordkeeping" means under Rules 17a-3/17a-4 — specifically whether AI-generated outputs and the prompts that produced them must be archived as business communications

White House · Policy

White House Reviewed AI Model Evaluation Framework With Major Labs — Then Refused to Publish It

On August 4, the White House convened a session with Meta, Nvidia, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, and smaller labs to review an AI model evaluation framework — and then declined to release it publicly. Fortune's reporting calls the decision "baffling." The framework's existence signals the government is developing safety benchmarks for frontier AI; its non-disclosure means those standards remain opaque to the firms expected to comply.

Why It Matters

When the government's internal safety standard for AI models is classified, regulated financial firms are left building governance frameworks without knowing what the public standard will eventually require. This creates a strategic decision: build conservatively now or wait for public guidance that may never arrive on a predictable timeline.

Skills · Compliance

AI Recordkeeping Is a Product Problem — PMs Who Understand Compliance Architecture Are Invaluable

PYMNTS reports financial services firms face a sharp regulatory grey zone: neither the SEC nor FINRA has yet ruled whether AI prompts and their outputs must be archived as business communications under Rules 17a-3 and 17a-4. The absence of a clear rulebook does not reduce the risk — existing supervision, recordkeeping, and fiduciary obligations apply regardless of whether a human or an AI system performed the underlying work.

Why It Matters

Compliance architecture is not the legal team's problem to solve alone — it is a product design decision about what your system logs, how long it retains that data, and whether a compliance team can interrogate it post-hoc without engineering support. PMs who own this design question are uniquely valuable at regulated firms.
